Understanding IRR performance in Java

From a historical solver to an optimized one, one change at a time

Internal rate of return is the root of a sum of discounted cashflows. This study rebuilds two period-IRR solvers in Java 21: a historical one (boxed pairs, one Math.pow per cashflow, Brent's method with a Newton fallback) and an optimized one (primitive arrays, a logarithmic coordinate with a multiplicative recurrence, analytic bounds that prove whether a root exists, Newton first from a rational guess). Twelve cumulative stages connect and extend them, and each stage is timed with JMH on seven classes of input.

  • Where the root lies outside the historical search bracket, the historical solver returns 48 of 64 roots above it and none below it. The optimized solver returns all of them.
  • The measurements exposed two weaknesses of the optimized design. An experiment that reorders the same gaps attributes about two thirds of the irregular-gap cost to unpredictable branches; a branch-free power table removes it. A logarithmic form of the Newton step cuts far-negative roots from 43 evaluations to 5.
  • On 16 hardware threads the extended solver reaches 11.7 million solves per second. It is 8 to 12 times faster than an open-source XIRR library on dated input, and Apache POI's periodic IRR, given a good guess, is faster when dates must be digested.

A resilient home lab on a small cluster

Turning an undocumented home network into one that survives losing a machine

A household network and its services (filtering DNS with recursive resolution, Wi-Fi management, metrics and dashboards) run on a three-node Raspberry Pi Kubernetes cluster. The first cluster was meant to keep services up when a node failed, but it did not: an inspection showed that its services and their storage were tied to single machines. It was rebuilt with a replicated control plane and three-way replicated storage, and every service was then tested by shutting down each production node in turn and checking that it recovered.

The case study will cover that rebuild, the failure tests and their retained evidence, and an open problem: intermittent network partitions on the nodes, where the current fix contains the symptom without proving the diagnosis.

Concordia: governed anonymity for group votes

An experimental protocol for anonymous voting with a threshold reveal

Members of a group prove they belong to it without revealing who they are, cast one ballot each, and results stay hidden until the vote closes. Identity can be revealed only through a pre-agreed ceremony in which two of three trustees cooperate, not by a database query. Accepted ballots go into a public hash-chained log that anyone can recount after the vote closes. The cryptographic core is written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly for the browser; a Telegram bot and a one-page browser demo are the two ways in.

The protocol is experimental and has not been externally reviewed. The case study will state its known weaknesses alongside what it does.
